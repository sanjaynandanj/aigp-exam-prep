# Soft Law: OECD, Council of Europe Framework Convention, UNESCO, G7 Hiroshima

**Phase 4 · Lesson 10 · ~1h · BoK Domain II**

## EXAM FOCUS

Soft law questions are gift points if you keep the instruments straight: **who issued it, is it binding, what's its signature feature**. The four stars: **OECD AI Principles** (the vocabulary source), **Council of Europe Framework Convention** (the *first binding international AI treaty* — the exception in this "soft" lesson), **UNESCO Recommendation** (broadest membership, ethics focus), and the **G7 Hiroshima Process** (GenAI code of conduct). Distinguish carefully: the exam loves "which of these is legally binding?"

## CORE

### Why soft law matters (the framing the exam expects)

- **Speed and reach:** non-binding instruments move faster than treaties/statutes and cross borders where hard law can't.
- **Convergence engine:** they seed shared vocabulary (the OECD definition of an AI system is inside the EU AI Act and US frameworks) — the interoperability layer for multinational programs.
- **Hard-law feeder:** today's principles become tomorrow's statutes (OECD → AI Act values; Hiroshima code → GPAI Code of Practice DNA).
- **Governance evidence:** adopting recognized principles demonstrates reasonable, good-faith governance to regulators, courts, and buyers.

### OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024)

| Attribute | Content |
|---|---|
| Status | **Non-binding Recommendation** of the OECD Council — the first intergovernmental AI standard; adherents beyond OECD membership (46+ countries); basis of the **G20 AI Principles** (2019) |
| **Five values-based principles** | 1. **Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being**; 2. **Human rights and democratic values, incl. fairness and privacy** (rule of law, autonomy — strengthened in the 2024 update); 3. **Transparency and explainability**; 4. **Robustness, security and safety**; 5. **Accountability** (incl. traceability; 2024 update added responsible business conduct, info-integrity and safety refinements) |
| **Five recommendations to governments** | R&D investment; inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem (data, compute, infrastructure); enabling interoperable governance/policy environment; **human capacity building & labor-market transition**; **international cooperation** |
| Signature exports | The **OECD definition of an AI system** (machine-based system inferring from inputs to generate outputs…) — adopted nearly verbatim by the EU AI Act and NIST-world documents; the **OECD.AI Policy Observatory** and the AI **incidents monitor**; lifecycle/actor classifications |

### Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (2024)

| Attribute | Content |
|---|---|
| Status | **THE FIRST LEGALLY BINDING INTERNATIONAL TREATY ON AI** — the one instrument in this lesson that is hard law once ratified. Opened for signature Sept 2024; open to **non-CoE states** (early signatories included the EU, US, UK, Israel, Japan-adjacent partners — verify ratification status as of mid-2026) |
| Focus | Activities within the **lifecycle of AI systems** that may interfere with **human rights, democracy, and the rule of law** — a rights treaty, not a product-safety law |
| Core obligations of parties | Ensure AI activities are consistent with: human dignity & autonomy; **transparency & oversight** (incl. identifying AI-generated content); **accountability**; **equality & non-discrimination**; **privacy & personal-data protection**; **reliability**; **safe innovation** (sandbox-friendly); provide **remedies** and procedural safeguards (incl. notice that one is interacting with AI); risk & impact management framework; option of bans/moratoria for incompatible uses |
| Flexibility/critique points | **Framework** convention: sets principles, leaves implementation methods to parties; applies fully to **public-sector** AI activities, with parties choosing how to address **private actors** (a negotiated compromise); **national-security/defence** carve-outs; no direct penalty machinery — follow-up via a **Conference of the Parties** |

### UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)

| Attribute | Content |
|---|---|
| Status | Non-binding recommendation adopted by **all UNESCO member states** (~194) — the **broadest global consensus** instrument, notable for including China and the Global South |
| Values/principles | Human rights & dignity, environment & ecosystem flourishing, diversity & inclusiveness, peaceful/just societies; proportionality & do-no-harm, safety/security, fairness & non-discrimination, sustainability, privacy, **human oversight & determination** (AI must never displace ultimate human responsibility), transparency & explainability, responsibility & accountability, awareness & literacy, multi-stakeholder governance |
| Distinctive features | **11 policy action areas**; operational tools: **Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM)** for states and **Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA)**; explicit **ban stance on social scoring and mass surveillance** uses incompatible with human rights; strong gender/environment chapters |

### G7 Hiroshima AI Process (2023)

| Attribute | Content |
|---|---|
| Status | G7 leaders' initiative on **generative/advanced AI**; produced the **International Guiding Principles** (11) and the **International Code of Conduct for Organizations Developing Advanced AI Systems** — voluntary, aimed directly at **frontier developers** |
| Code-of-conduct themes | Risk identification/evaluation/mitigation across the lifecycle (incl. red-teaming); post-deployment **incident monitoring**; public **transparency reports** on capabilities/limitations; governance & risk-management policies; security controls; **provenance/labeling of AI-generated content** (watermarking-type mechanisms); research into societal risks; priority on global challenges; international standards work; data protection & IP safeguards |
| Trajectory | Fed the EU **GPAI Code of Practice** and company frontier-safety commitments; an OECD-supported **reporting framework** for code adherence followed (2025) — mention level |

### UN efforts & the safety-summit track (recognition level)

- **UN General Assembly resolution (March 2024)** "Seizing the opportunities of safe, secure and trustworthy AI…" — first UNGA AI resolution, consensus-adopted (US-led, China co-sponsored); non-binding.
- UN **High-Level Advisory Body on AI** → *Governing AI for Humanity* (2024): proposals incl. an **International Scientific Panel on AI** and **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** (endorsed via the **Global Digital Compact**, 2024; operationalization ongoing — verify status).
- **AI Safety Summit lineage:** Bletchley Declaration (2023, 28 states + EU incl. US & China on frontier risk) → Seoul (2024, company frontier-safety commitments) → Paris AI **Action** Summit (2025, broader innovation/inclusion framing) — a soft-law thread on **frontier-model safety** and state-backed **AI safety/security institutes**.

### Comparison table — the exam grid

| Instrument | Issuer/year | Binding? | Scope focus | Signature feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI Principles | OECD 2019/2024 | No | General AI policy | Source of the global AI **definition**; 5+5 structure |
| Framework Convention | Council of Europe 2024 | **Yes (treaty, once ratified)** | Human rights, democracy, rule of law | **First binding international AI treaty**; open beyond Europe; public-sector-first |
| Ethics Recommendation | UNESCO 2021 | No | Ethics, development, inclusion | ~194 states; RAM/EIA tools; anti-social-scoring stance |
| Hiroshima Principles + Code | G7 2023 | No | Advanced/generative AI developers | Frontier-developer code: red-teaming, provenance, transparency reports |
| UNGA resolution / GDC bodies | UN 2024 | No | Global capacity & bridge-building | Universality; scientific panel + global dialogue |

## CASES & EXAMPLES

**Scenario 1 — "Which is binding?"** A question lists the OECD Principles, UNESCO Recommendation, Hiroshima Code, and the CoE Framework Convention. *Analysis:* Only the **Council of Europe Framework Convention** is a treaty creating legal obligations for ratifying parties. Everything else is persuasive soft law — however influential.

**Scenario 2 — The definitional through-line.** A policy analyst notices the EU AI Act's "AI system" definition matches language in US guidance. *Analysis:* Both derive from the **OECD definition** — deliberate alignment to enable regulatory interoperability. Exam point: OECD is the *vocabulary* layer of global AI governance.

**Scenario 3 — Frontier-lab commitments.** A model developer promises red-team testing, incident monitoring, transparency reports on limitations, and watermark-style provenance for generated content. *Analysis:* That's the **G7 Hiroshima Code of Conduct** checklist (echoed in the EU GPAI Code of Practice) — voluntary commitments that map cleanly onto later binding GPAI duties.

**Scenario 4 — Advising a government.** A developing country wants to gauge its preparedness for AI ethics obligations and assess a proposed national AI project's societal impact. *Analysis:* UNESCO's toolkit: the **Readiness Assessment Methodology** for the state's ecosystem and the **Ethical Impact Assessment** for the project — the operational arms of the Recommendation.

## MEMORY HOOKS

- **Issuer one-liners:** *OECD defines, CoE binds, UNESCO includes, G7 restrains the frontier, UN convenes.*
- **Binding trap:** "Framework Convention" sounds soft — it's the **hard** one. Everything called "principles/recommendation/code" is soft.
- **OECD 5 values mnemonic — "I-H-T-R-A":** *"**I** **H**ave **T**rustworthy **R**obust **A**I"* → Inclusive growth, Human rights/fairness, Transparency, Robustness/safety, Accountability.
- **Hiroshima = GenAI's rulebook draft:** red-team, report, watermark, monitor.
- **Date pegs:** OECD 2019 → UNESCO 2021 → Hiroshima/Bletchley 2023 → CoE treaty + UNGA 2024.

**The 5 facts to retain:**
1. Soft law's exam role: convergence vocabulary, speed, and the feeder pipeline into binding rules (OECD definition → EU AI Act; Hiroshima → GPAI Code of Practice).
2. OECD AI Principles (2019, upd. 2024): five values-based principles + five government recommendations; source of the near-universal AI-system definition; basis for G20's version.
3. The **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI** (2024) is the **first legally binding international AI treaty** — rights/democracy/rule-of-law focused, open to non-European states, public-sector-first with private-sector flexibility.
4. UNESCO's Recommendation (2021) has the widest adoption (~194 states), adds RAM and Ethical Impact Assessment tools, and insists on ultimate **human responsibility**.
5. The G7 Hiroshima Process produced voluntary guiding principles and a **code of conduct for advanced-AI developers** (red-teaming, incident monitoring, provenance labeling, transparency reporting).

## SELF-CHECK

1. **Q:** Which international AI instrument is legally binding, and on whom?
   **A:** The Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI, Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law — binding on states that sign and ratify it (including possible non-CoE parties), obliging them to implement its principles, primarily for public-sector AI activities.

2. **Q:** List the five OECD values-based principles.
   **A:** Inclusive growth/sustainable development/well-being; human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability.

3. **Q:** What two operational assessment tools accompany the UNESCO Recommendation?
   **A:** The Readiness Assessment Methodology (country-level preparedness) and the Ethical Impact Assessment (project/system-level).

4. **Q:** What does the G7 Hiroshima Code of Conduct ask of advanced-AI developers? Name four commitments.
   **A:** Lifecycle risk assessment incl. red-teaming; post-deployment monitoring and incident reporting; public transparency reports on capabilities/limits; security controls; content-provenance/labeling mechanisms (e.g., watermarking); governance policies; research on societal risks (any four).

5. **Q:** Give two concrete examples of soft law shaping hard law in AI governance.
   **A:** The OECD's AI-system definition adopted into the EU AI Act (and Colorado/US usage); the Hiroshima Code of Conduct informing the EU's GPAI Code of Practice (and, more broadly, OECD principles shaping the AI Act's trustworthy-AI requirements and Colorado's framework-presumption approach).
